The system

How The Finder’s Book works

Five steps, in the order that produces something usable soonest. The first four decisions take about fifteen minutes, and a half-finished book is already better than the alternative.

Step 1

Organize what exists.

You are not writing your life story. You are building an index to it.

The book walks through the areas of a life that someone else may suddenly have to understand: the people who matter, the money, the property, the medical picture, the household, and the digital accounts. For each one, the question is the same and it is deliberately small — what exists, and where would somebody look?

That framing matters more than it sounds. “List your assets” is a project people abandon. “Which bank holds the everyday account?” is a question anyone can answer in four seconds. The whole book is built out of the second kind of question. If you want the full inventory of what belongs in that index, we wrote a companion guide to the important documents your family should be able to find.

Step 2

Point to where protected information lives — never copy it.

This is the decision that shapes everything else, and it is why the finished book is far less dangerous to keep at home than a list of credentials would be.

A vault holds your secrets. A pointer holds directions to your secrets. The Finder’s Book is structurally a pointer. It records which bank, not which password. Which attorney, not which account number. Where the will is kept, not what the will says. Which password manager the household uses and who has been told how to reach it — never the master password itself.

What the book records

  • Who to call first, and who backs them up
  • Where the signed originals are physically kept
  • Which institutions hold what, by name
  • Last four digits, for identification only
  • Who is named as executor, agent, or guardian
  • A pointer to the secure system that holds the secrets

What the book never records

  • Passwords for any account
  • PINs for any card or device
  • Two-factor recovery codes
  • Full account numbers
  • Full Social Security numbers
  • Combinations to safes or storage units

That constraint costs a little convenience and buys a great deal of safety. A vault found by the wrong person is a catastrophe; a pointer found by the wrong person is a far smaller problem — but not nothing. Names, institutions and record locations still deserve protecting, so store the finished book somewhere secure that your Finder can reach.

Step 3

Name the people who may need to know.

We call the person who may one day have to step in your Finder. Your Finder might be your spouse, an adult child, a sibling, your closest friend, a caregiver, an executor, or your chosen family. What matters is not the relationship. What matters is that the role is decided in advance, by you, rather than defaulting to whoever happens to be in the room.

The book asks you to name a first call and a backup, and to record the professionals and institutions that already hold pieces of the picture — the attorney, the accountant, the physician, the insurer, the employer. Your Finder does not need to know everything you know. They need to know who does.

Step 4

Create a safer handoff.

A finished book that nobody can find has solved nothing. The handoff is part of the system, not an afterthought.

  • Decide where the book physically lives, and write that down somewhere separate
  • Tell your Finder it exists and where it is — a sentence is enough
  • Keep the truly sensitive material where it already belongs: the safe, the password manager, the attorney’s file
  • Store the original legal documents according to your attorney’s instructions, not in the binder

Because the book is a pointer, this conversation is far easier than it would otherwise be. You are not handing anyone the keys to your life. You are telling them where the map is.

Step 5

Keep it current.

Information decays. A book that was accurate three years ago can send someone to a bank you left and an attorney who retired.

Rather than a calendar reminder most people ignore, the book ties review to events that already get your attention: a move, a new job, a marriage or divorce, a death in the family, a new diagnosis, a change of attorney or accountant, a new mortgage or insurer. When one of those happens, one page usually needs a line changed. That is the whole maintenance burden.

What this is not

It complements your legal and medical planning. It does not replace it.

The Finder’s Book is a structured template for personal record-keeping. It is not a will, a trust, a contract, a power of attorney, or an advance directive, and it confers no authority over any account, property, or medical decision. The legal documents your family will need are the originals, executed and stored according to the requirements of your jurisdiction.

What the book does is make sure someone knows those documents exist, where they are, and who to call about them. A beautifully prepared estate plan does not help a family that cannot find it.
