Family emergency planning: what the person stepping in actually needs

Most emergency planning advice is written for the person making the plan. This is written for the person who has to use it — usually at speed, usually upset, usually without you there to ask.

What it is

It is not estate planning, and confusing the two costs families time.

Estate planning decides what happens: who inherits, who has authority, what your wishes are. It is legal work, and it produces legal documents — a will, a trust, a power of attorney, an advance directive.

Family emergency planning decides what someone can find. It is not legal work. It produces an index: which institutions hold what, who to call in what order, where the signed originals are kept, and what obligations continue whether or not anyone is paying attention to them.

Families who have done the first without the second are extremely common, and it is a genuinely frustrating place to be. The plan exists. It is correct. It is signed. And for three weeks nobody can find it, because the only person who knew where it was kept is the person the plan is about.

The three situations

One plan has to survive three very different days.

People tend to prepare for death and skip the two situations that are far more likely to happen first.

A sudden emergency

  • You are expected to recover
  • Someone needs to reach the right people today
  • Bills, pets, dependants, and work continue regardless
  • Nothing legal has changed — this is pure logistics

Extended incapacity

  • Decisions have to be made on your behalf, for weeks or longer
  • Authority now matters — who is actually allowed to act
  • Medical wishes need to be known, not guessed
  • Money has to keep moving: mortgage, insurance, payroll

The third is death, and it is the one everybody pictures. It is also the one where the search is longest, because there is no longer anyone to ask a clarifying question. A plan built only for that day tends to be too legal and too slow to help on the other two.

The first 48 hours

The questions that get asked, in roughly the order they get asked.

If you want a fast test of whether your household is prepared, hand this list to the person most likely to be called and see how many they can answer without you.

  • Who needs to be told, and in what order?
  • Who is legally allowed to make decisions right now — medical, and financial?
  • Where are the signed originals of those authorities kept?
  • Is there health insurance, life insurance, or disability cover, and with whom?
  • Which employer, and who there needs to know?
  • What has to be paid this month regardless, and from which account?
  • Who depends on this household — children, an older parent, an animal?
  • Which professionals already know something useful: attorney, accountant, physician, adviser?
  • What is going to keep happening automatically, and who can stop it?

Nobody enjoys that list. But a household where someone can answer most of it has already removed the worst part of the experience: the searching, done by someone who is frightened, at the exact moment when they have the least capacity for it.

Where to start

Four decisions carry most of the weight.

The reason most emergency-planning projects stall is that they ask for eighty answers before returning anything useful. People start, stall around page six, and never come back to a document that now makes them feel behind.

Front-load these four instead. Fifteen minutes here is worth more than an unfinished afternoon spread across everything else.

  • Name the first call. One person, and a backup, both told in advance that this is their role.
  • Record where the originals live. Not what they say — where they physically are.
  • List the professionals and institutions. By name, with a phone number that works.
  • Write down what continues. The obligations that do not pause for a crisis.

Everything beyond that is depth, added whenever real life allows. The next layer is usually the paperwork itself — there is a category-by-category walkthrough in our guide to the important documents your family should be able to find.

The safety rule

Do not build a document that is dangerous to lose.

There is an obvious temptation when you start doing this: put everything in one place so nobody has to search. Passwords, PINs, account numbers, the safe combination, where the cash is. It feels thorough.

It also creates a single object that hands over your entire financial life to whoever picks it up — and an emergency is precisely when that object gets left on a kitchen table, photographed, emailed, or carried between houses by people who are not thinking about security.

The safer construction is to record where protected information lives and leave the information itself inside the systems built to protect it. That is the principle the whole of our safer information handoff is built on, and it is what makes the finished document safe to keep in an ordinary drawer.
