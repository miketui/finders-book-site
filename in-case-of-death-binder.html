In case of death binder: what goes in it, and what must never

A death binder is one findable place that answers the questions your family will actually be asked in the first week. Built well, it is a map. Built badly, it is a stolen identity waiting to happen.

What it is

One place, findable without you, that says where everything is.

People call it different things — an in case of death binder, a death binder, an estate planning binder, a legacy binder, a “when I’m gone” folder. The name does not matter. The job does: when someone dies or is suddenly unable to speak for themselves, the people around them are asked a stream of specific questions, immediately, by institutions that will not wait.

Who is the executor. Which funeral home. Is there a prepaid plan. Where is the deed. Which bank holds the mortgage. Who is the insurance carrier and what is the policy number. Was there a pension. Does anyone have the keys to the storage unit.

None of those questions are hard for you. All of them are nearly impossible for someone who has to reconstruct your life from a filing cabinet while grieving. The binder exists to close that gap, and nothing else.

A binder is a record-keeping tool. It is not a will, trust, power of attorney, or advance directive, and it grants no authority to anyone. The documents that carry legal effect are the originals, executed and stored as your jurisdiction requires. Consult a qualified professional about anything with legal, medical, or financial consequence.

This is not an end of life planner in the legal or medical sense. It can point to executed documents and record practical wishes, but it does not create, amend, or replace those documents.

The contents

Five sections, in the order the questions arrive.

  • People. Who to call first and who backs them up. The executor or personal representative. Attorney, accountant, financial advisor, insurance agent. Employer or HR contact. The friend or neighbour with a key.
  • Documents. Where the originals physically live — will, trust, deeds, titles, birth and marriage certificates, military discharge papers, divorce decrees, citizenship papers. Where, not what. A binder records the location of the safe deposit box and who is authorized on it; it does not reproduce the will.
  • Money in and money out. Which institutions hold accounts, which bills are on autopay and from where, insurance policies and pensions, outstanding loans, subscriptions that will keep charging a dead person’s card. Institution names and last four digits — never full numbers.
  • Home and daily operations. Utilities and account holders, the alarm company, landlord or HOA, vehicles and where the titles are, pets and their vet, the storage unit nobody remembers.
  • Wishes. Burial or cremation, any prepaid arrangement and with whom, organ donation, service preferences, who should be told and by whom, what you want said. This section carries no legal force, and it is often the one that spares a family the worst argument.

Most of a first pass takes an evening. The full picture is worth building over a few sittings — but an unfinished binder that names the executor and the funeral wishes already outperforms a perfect one that was never started.

The rule that decides everything

Build a pointer, not a vault.

The instinct is to make the binder complete: passwords, PINs, account numbers, the safe combination, all in one place so nobody is ever stuck. That instinct produces a document that hands a stranger the whole household in a single theft — from a drawer, a house fire cleanup crew, a break-in, an estate sale, a contractor.

Safe to write down

  • Institution and professional names
  • Where an original document is kept
  • Last four digits, for identification
  • Who holds a role, and how to reach them
  • Which password manager the household uses
  • Whether legacy or recovery contacts are set up

Never write down

  • Passwords or master passwords
  • PINs for cards or devices
  • Two-factor or account recovery codes
  • Full account or card numbers
  • Full Social Security numbers
  • Safe combinations, or where cash is hidden

A binder built only from the left-hand column is genuinely useful to your family and close to worthless to whoever else finds it. It still holds private personal information, so it still belongs somewhere secure — but the consequences of it being found are survivable, which is the whole design goal. The same logic is set out in a safer information handoff.

Where to keep it

A binder nobody can find is the same as no binder.

Two failures are equally common: kept so securely that no one can reach it, or kept so casually that anyone can. Aim between them — a locked drawer or home safe, with at least two people told it exists and how to get to it.

The safe deposit box trap. A box can be sealed or effectively inaccessible after a death until the right paperwork exists, and the paperwork is often what is inside the box. Keep the box, and keep a record of where it is and who is authorized on it somewhere your family can reach without it.

Paper, digital, or both. Paper survives a dead phone, a forgotten password, and a locked account, and it can be handed to someone in a hospital corridor. A digital copy survives a house fire and can be updated without reprinting. Many households keep a printed binder at home and a PDF in the password manager the family already uses. Whichever you choose, tell someone.

Say it out loud once. The single highest-value step is not the binder — it is one sentence to the person who will need it: “if something happens, the binder is in the desk, and here is how you get into it.”

Why most binders fail

Not discipline. Design.

Binders assembled from a blank pack of dividers usually stall for the same three reasons. They start with the hardest section, so the first evening produces nothing usable. They are open-ended, so there is no point at which they are done enough. And they were built once, then quietly went out of date — a bank that was sold, an executor who moved, an insurer that no longer exists.

Fixes that work: put the four decisions that matter in an emergency on the first page, so fifteen minutes is already worth something. Treat the rest as optional depth. And tie updates to events rather than dates — a move, a new job, a marriage or divorce, a death in the family, a new diagnosis, a new mortgage or insurer. When one happens, usually one line changes.

If you want the underlying inventory rather than the container, the important documents checklist covers what to record for each category, and family emergency planning covers the version of this that matters while everyone is still alive.

Common questions

What people ask before they build one.

What should be in an in case of death binder?

Five sections, in the order the questions arrive: who to call, where the original documents are kept, which institutions hold money coming in and going out, how the home runs day to day, and your funeral and end-of-life wishes. Record locations and institution names rather than account numbers or credentials.

What should never go in a death binder?

Passwords and master passwords, card and device PINs, two-factor or account recovery codes, full account or card numbers, full Social Security numbers, and safe combinations. A binder built from locations helps your family; a binder built from credentials hands a stranger the whole household at once.

Where should an in case of death binder be kept?

Somewhere secure that your family can actually reach, such as a locked drawer or home safe, with at least two people told it exists and how to open it. Avoid making a safe deposit box the only copy, because a box can be difficult to access after a death and the paperwork needed to open it is often inside.

Is a death binder the same as a will?

No. A will is a legal instrument that decides who inherits and is executed under the rules of your jurisdiction. A binder is a record-keeping tool that tells people where things are and who to contact. It carries no legal authority and never replaces a will, trust, power of attorney, or advance directive.

Should the binder be paper or digital?

Both works best. Paper survives a dead phone, a forgotten password, and a locked account, and it can be handed to someone in person. A digital copy survives a house fire and is easier to keep current. Many households keep a printed copy at home and a PDF in the password manager the family already uses.
